0. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
================================================================================

The idea in one line (your words, cleaned): a user gives reference images of characters plus a story prompt, optionally a template and specific scenes, and gets back a 60 to 120 second microdrama episode that is as engaging as the real thing. Python only, Colab fine, built on the Magic Hour API, with a benchmark that reruns the same inputs so every iteration is comparable.

What I verified today in Magic Hour's own docs [R1 R2 R3 R4 R5 R6 R7 R9]:
- Text-to-video and image-to-video exist, with 14 listed models (ltx-2.5, minimax-h3, wan-2.2, wan-3.0, kling-2.6, kling-3.0, veo3.1, veo3.1-lite, seedance-1.5, seedance-2.0, seedance-2.0-mini, seedance-2.5, sora-2, gemini-omni-1.1) [R3].
- Text-to-video accepts a "references" array of {name, file_path} [R1]. Image-to-video accepts a start image and an optional end image [R2]. Both are the hooks for character consistency. Limits on reference count and how prompts address a reference by name are NOT documented on the pages I read. That is the biggest unknown and the first experiment.
- Max single clip length depends on model: kling-3.0 15s, seedance-2.0 15s, seedance-2.5 30s, minimax-h3 30s, ltx-2.5 60s, sora-2 60s, veo3.1 up to 56s in fixed steps [R1 R3].
- Native audio exists on several models, with or without extra credits [R3]. Separate lip-sync (1 credit per frame, doubled on pro) [R3 R6], AI voice generator with "celebrity and character voices" at 0.1 credit per character [R3 R7], voice cloner at 0.1 per character [R3], audio-to-video [R8], saved "characters, references, voices, moodboards" items via the API [R9].
- 9:16 is supported on the models listed in the docs I read (partially truncated in the fetch, so verify per model in the first test) [R1].
- Typical processing times: text-to-video p50 25s, image-to-video p50 33s, lip-sync p50 1m30s (30 day window to 16 Sep 2026) [R4].
- No API concurrency cap, jobs can queue [R9]. Credits are refunded on failed jobs [R1].

The number that shapes everything (my arithmetic from R3, Table C1 in Section 4):
- One 60 second episode costs 1,440 credits at the cheapest tier (ltx-2.5, 480p), 2,880 at ltx-2.5 720p, 4,320 with kling-3.0 at 720p with audio, and 17,280 with seedance-2.0 at 720p.
- 20,000 credits therefore buy roughly 12 episodes at the cheapest quality and zero to two with the premium models once rerolls are counted. This is a prototype budget, not a production budget.

Verdict of the stress test (Section 1.9): the idea survives as "a story engine, shot planner, consistency manager and benchmark harness on top of a third-party video API". It does not survive as "reaches the best human microdramas using only 20k credits". The fixes are: draft cheap then finalize selectively, make the benchmark the product, keep the backend pluggable, and choose a clear wedge (Hindi and Indian-language microdramas, with reference-image characters and measured quality).

What I recommend you decide first (Section 12 has all questions): which Magic Hour plan your 20k credits sit on (free tier locks kling and seedance), whether v1 is a demo or a product, and which languages v1 must cover.

1. SKEPTICAL STRESS TEST
================================================================================

You asked me to act as a sharp, skeptical reviewer: first build the strongest case against the idea, then say whether it survives, then show where it breaks and how to fix it. If it is solid, say so, and do not invent flaws. I follow that. Each attack below has: the attack, the evidence, severity (1 low to 5 fatal-if-ignored), whether the idea survives it, and the fix.

1.0 The claim being tested
Claim C: "With reference images, a prompt, templates and scene instructions, our pipeline on Magic Hour's API can produce a microdrama episode of 60 to 120 seconds that is as good as the best real microdramas and shorts, measured by a repeatable benchmark."
Claim C has four parts that fail independently: (a) the story is as good, (b) the characters, voices and style stay consistent, (c) it is affordable to iterate, (d) the measurement is trustworthy. I attack each.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTACK A1. The credit budget cannot support the iteration the claim needs.   Severity 5
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Attack: Quality comes from iteration and rerolls. 20,000 credits is a thin budget for a video pipeline.
Evidence: Magic Hour bills video per second of output, rates in R3: ltx-2.5 24 credits/s at 480p and 48 at 720p; kling-3.0 48 at 720p plus 24 for audio; seedance-2.0 288 at 720p; seedance-2.5 576 at 720p; veo3.1 96 at 720p plus 24 for audio. A 60s episode at 1.0x (no rerolls) is 1,440 to 34,560 credits depending on model (Table C1). With a realistic acceptance rate of 40 to 60 percent of generated shot-seconds, the cheapest tier gives 5 to 7 full episodes and the premium tiers give 0 to 1 (Table C2). The credit-to-dollar rate in the pricing page text is about 400 to 533 credits per dollar depending on pack [R10], so 20,000 credits is about 38 to 50 dollars of value. This is my arithmetic from the pages cited.
Does the idea survive? Partly. It survives as a prototype that proves the pipeline and the benchmark. It does not survive as a method to reach top-tier quality on every iteration.
Fix:
1. Two-pass generation. Draft every shot cheaply (ltx-2.5 at 480p, 24 credits/s) to verify framing, action and consistency, then render only approved shots on a better model. Table C3 shows this lets about 3 to 5 final episodes fit in 20k.
2. Never reroll a whole episode. Reroll at shot level, with a shot-level automated pre-check (face match against the reference, black frame, text artifacts) before spending on lip-sync or upscale.
3. Cache everything by content hash: prompts, reference images, model, seed, duration. A benchmark rerun must reuse unchanged shots, so a script change costs credits only for changed shots.
4. Treat generation budget as a first-class variable in the benchmark (credits per accepted minute), so improvements that cost 5x do not count as wins.
5. Use the free tier, mock server and account endpoint to build and test the orchestration with zero credit spend [R3 R9]. The docs list a mock server and a balance endpoint (GET /v1/account).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTACK A2. Character consistency across 6 to 12 separate clips is still an open research problem.   Severity 5
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Attack: The claim depends on the same actor face, outfit and body appearing across many independently generated clips. Single-clip generators drift. Even papers from 2026 treat this as unsolved.
Evidence: EntityBench, GroundShot, PersonaShot, EM-Vid, VideoMemory, InfinityStory and SubjectAnchor are 2026 papers whose stated motivation is that entity consistency across shots remains a challenge [P1 P2 P4 P5 P6 P7 P22]. GroundShot says inconsistencies accumulate as the number of shots grows [P2]. PersonaShot (Aug 2026) is a benchmark built because person-centric narrative continuity is not solved [P4]. These are abstract-level readings.
Magic Hour-specific gap: the text-to-video "references" field exists [R1], but nothing I read states the maximum count, how references are addressed in the prompt, or whether identity is preserved when two references share a frame. Image-to-video carries the identity in the first frame but identity can drift during motion and in later shots.
Does the idea survive? Yes, if consistency is engineered in the pipeline instead of assumed from the model.
Fix (the "anchor stack"):
1. Character bible per person: a canonical front, three-quarter and profile image, a wardrobe sheet, and a fixed text identity string (age, build, hair, wardrobe colors) pasted verbatim into every shot prompt.
2. Use the references array on every shot that features the character. If references work well, prefer text-to-video with references for establishing shots.
3. Use image-to-video with first-frame and last-frame images for continuity: the last frame of shot N (or a still produced by an image editor from it) becomes the first frame of shot N+1 when the scene continues.
4. Limit visible characters per shot to two where possible, close-ups for dialogue. Fewer faces reduce drift and cost less to verify.
5. Automatic identity check per shot: face embeddings (open source InsightFace class models, see Section 7) compared with the reference; reject shots below a threshold before any further spend.
6. A Magic Hour face-swap step is a last resort repair (video face swap costs 24 credits/s [R3]) and has ethics and quality caveats.
7. Benchmark metric: identity similarity per shot and across shot pairs (Section 10).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTACK A3. Voice consistency and lip sync across clips.   Severity 4
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Attack: If each clip generates its own audio, each clip can invent a slightly different voice for the same character. Your own criterion lists "consistency of voice" first.
Evidence: Native audio is listed per model [R3] with no statement about cross-clip voice identity. The AI Voice Generator is described as "celebrity and character voices" [R7], which creates impersonation risk and may not offer Indian-language voices (the page I read does not list languages). The Voice Cloner exists (0.1 credit per character) [R3] but needs a voice sample. Lip sync costs 1 credit per frame, doubled on pro [R3 R6], about 24 credits/s standard.
Does the idea survive? Yes, with a decoupled audio design.
Fix:
1. Generate dialogue audio separately per character with one fixed voice per character (Magic Hour voice cloner from a consented sample, or open source TTS such as IndicF5 for Indian languages [R65], CosyVoice [R66], IndexTTS [R64], Qwen3-TTS [P33]).
2. Generate shot video with audio off, or discard its audio, then apply lip-sync to dialogue close-ups only, or use audio-to-video [R8].
3. Keep music and ambience as a separate bed so shot-to-shot sound seams are covered.
4. Benchmark: speaker similarity between lines of the same character (speaker embedding cosine), plus a human 1 to 5 rating.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTACK A4. Clip length ceilings force seams, and seams break the "microdrama feel".   Severity 3
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Attack: Real microdramas hold performance across 20 to 40 second continuous scenes. API clips top out at 15 seconds on kling-3.0, seedance-2.0 and wan-2.2, 30 on seedance-2.5, minimax-h3 and wan-3.0, up to 60 on ltx-2.5 and sora-2 [R1 R3].
Evidence: R1/R3 duration tables.
Does the idea survive? Yes. Real microdramas are cut fast. Typical shot length in the format is short; your own default of 60 seconds suggests 8 to 14 shots of 4 to 8 seconds. The cut itself is a feature (cliffhanger rhythm).
Fix: design the shot plan for 3 to 8 second shots, hard cuts, reaction inserts, and J and L cuts using the continuous audio bed. Avoid long single takes. Use the longer-clip models only for establishing or action shots where identity risk is low.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTACK A5. "As good as the best real microdramas" is an undefined and probably unreachable bar for the whole stack.   Severity 4
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Attack: The best microdramas succeed through casting, acting, pacing and script craft learned from thousands of tested episodes, plus distribution machinery. A generator that copies surface structure gets formulaic output. The market is also now flooded with AI output, so "watchable" is table stakes.
Evidence: More than 50,000 AI-native titles hit Douyin in March 2026 alone [R24]. AI microdramas were over 95 percent of new releases in Q1 2026 by one industry data set (C/B grade, Chinese-language, secondary) [R19]. Micro-drama platforms were already dropping paywalls as viewers lost interest in 2025 [R30], which signals fatigue with formulaic content. Sensor Tower reports downloads up 140 percent year on year while revenue grew 20 percent, so monetisation per download is under pressure [R14 R15].
Does the idea survive? Partly. The practical bar should be "beats the median AI microdrama and approaches the bottom quartile of human-made hits on the same script", and the benchmark should measure the gap to a human-made reference set.
Fix:
1. Define the bar as a measured score on a frozen rubric plus pairwise preference against 5 reference microdramas you pick (Section 10).
2. Invest in the writers' room (script engine) more than the renderer. The renderer is a rented commodity; the script and shot plan are the differentiator (Section 2).
3. Build a "hook library" and "cliffhanger library" by deconstructing real episodes (shot-by-shot beat sheets for 20 to 30 episodes you choose), then use them as retrieval examples for the writer agent (Section 2.3).
4. Avoid cloning copyrighted plots. Use structure, not text (Section 9).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTACK A6. The evaluation plan is circular and n=1.   Severity 4
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Attack: "I will judge by watching" and "the model judges" are both weak. One human rater with one mood, and an LLM judge that prefers its own style, give noisy measurements, and noise looks like progress.
Evidence: 2026 papers keep proposing rubric-based and agentic judges because single-score judgments are unreliable: RAVEN-Eval, VideoArgus, FIRM-Video, DirectorBench, NarrLV [P40 to P44]. PlaylistEval (Sep 2026) specifically asks whether video-language judges can be trusted on long videos [P45]. A WWW 2026 paper studies MLLM-based short drama quality assessment [P46]. I have only read abstracts.
Does the idea survive? Yes if the benchmark is treated as an instrument that itself must be calibrated.
Fix:
1. Frozen benchmark set of 8 to 12 briefs (multi-genre, 2 to 3 languages), frozen rubric, frozen judge prompts and model versions, logged seeds.
2. Three layers: deterministic checks (structure, timing, identity similarity, audio loudness, caption safe area), model judges (script-level and video-level, temperature 0, 3 repeats, report mean and spread), and human pairwise comparisons for each release candidate (A versus B blind, 5 minutes).
3. Calibrate the judge against your human picks: compute rank correlation (Spearman) on 20 pairs. Only trust the judge dimensions that correlate above 0.6.
4. Include known-good and known-bad anchors: a human-made microdrama clip and a deliberately bad AI clip, so the scale does not drift.
5. Report uncertainty. Do not claim a win below the noise floor you measured.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTACK A7. Script quality and episode quality are different things, and the script engine cannot be judged in isolation.   Severity 3
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Attack: Great hooks on paper die if the first three seconds are visually weak. A script that is good for text can be unfilmable.
Evidence: structural scoring only measures what is written. The stub-LLM benchmark I built earlier tests structure only (my own test, no story quality claim).
Does the idea survive? Yes.
Fix: the writer agent must output filmable shots with explicit first-frame descriptions, and a separate "director pass" rewrites any shot the video model is known to fail at (crowds, hands, text, fast fight choreography) into safer coverage. Keep a "model failure catalogue" updated from benchmark runs.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTACK A8. Dependence on one vendor whose API is beta and whose model list changes.   Severity 3
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Evidence: the OpenAPI header says "version: beta" [R9 page]; the "default" model "tracks our current recommendation and changes over time" [R3]; legacy models are being retired from the docs [R3]. Models and prices can change under you mid-benchmark.
Does the idea survive? Yes.
Fix: pin the model ID in every call (never "default"), record model, resolution and docs snapshot hash in each benchmark run, keep the VideoBackend interface so Kling, Seedance, Veo or open source Wan can be swapped (Section 2.8).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTACK A9. "Everything free" collides with video generation.   Severity 3
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Attack: Free Colab cannot run top open video models. The free Magic Hour tier restricts models and resolution.
Evidence: Magic Hour says free plan models are ltx-2.5 and minimax-h3 at 480p; kling-3.0 and seedance-2.5 need a paid plan [R3]. Open source video models commonly need 24 to 48 GB VRAM for good output (Wan 2.2 5B on a 24 GB card, larger models on 48 GB) (C-grade guides) [R51]. Colab free GPUs are far below that (my knowledge of Colab tiers, not verified today).
Does the idea survive? Yes, because the 20k credits already exist. No new spend is required by this plan. But if those credits are free-tier starter credits, then kling and seedance may be locked. This is question Q5 in Section 12.
Fix: call GET /v1/account first thing once the key exists, to read balance and plan [R3]. Build the whole pipeline to run on ltx-2.5 and minimax-h3 at 480p first. Do not purchase anything without your explicit approval.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTACK A10. Real-person likeness, celebrity voices, and labelling rules.   Severity 4
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Attack: Reference images of real people can make a deepfake. Magic Hour's voice generator advertises celebrity voices [R7]. India's 2026 amendments require labelling of synthetically generated information, and platforms add their own labelling and reach limits.
Evidence: MeitY amendment rules notified 10 Feb 2026, effective 20 Feb 2026, introduce "synthetically generated information" duties [R72 R73 R74 R75]. YouTube's July 2025 "inauthentic content" update targets mass-produced or repetitive content [R76]. Instagram's AI label changes dated 31 Aug 2026 are from a secondary source (C) [R77]. Section 9 has details.
Does the idea survive? Yes with guardrails.
Fix: consent attestation for any real-person reference image, block celebrity and politician likeness and voice by default, embed visible and metadata labels (C2PA-style provenance is a stretch goal), a content-safety checklist in the benchmark, never publish real-person output without the owner's consent.

ATTACK A11. There is no moat: incumbents and many startups already sell "script to short drama".   Severity 4
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Evidence: Pexo, Medeo, OpenDrama, Pippit, aishortdrama.io, Drama.Land, Higgsfield, LTX Studio, OpenArt and Fable Showrunner all advertise some form of prompt or script to short drama [R36 to R47]. Open source: Jellyfish, Wind Comic, Open-AI-Micro-Drama-Generator, PenShot, huobao-drama, PureVis [R54 to R59]. ByteDance runs a closed loop from novels to models to distribution [R22]. Does the idea survive? As a learning project and portfolio piece, strongly. As a commercial product, only with a wedge.
Fix: pick a wedge. Candidates: (1) Indian languages and Indian genres, where India is a $300M market projected much higher but with a visible shake-out (Pocket FM closed Pocket TV in June 2026) [R26 R27 R28]; (2) measured quality: publish the benchmark and show the score curve, which almost no vendor does (D6 is zero for nearly all products in Table S2); (3) reference-image-first workflows for real creators and brands.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTACK A12. "Any language" is a claim, not a feature.   Severity 3
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Evidence: Text quality of LLMs in Hindi and regional languages varies; TTS and lip-sync models are mostly trained on Chinese and English with partial coverage elsewhere. Open source IndicF5 covers 11 Indian languages [R65]; IndexTTS had an open request for Hindi in Sep 2025 [R64 issue 429]; Qwen3-TTS and X-Voice target multilingual use [P33 P34]. Native lip-sync quality per language is unverified.
Does the idea survive? Yes, if v1 declares supported languages and tests each.
Fix: v1 languages: English and Hindi, tested with the benchmark; others flagged "experimental" until a per-language score exists. Dubbing from an English master is a fallback path (CineDub, Aug 2026, is relevant research [P36]).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ATTACK A13. Latency and hangs.   Severity 2
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Evidence: p50 25s text-to-video, 33s image-to-video, 1m30s lip-sync [R4]; p50 is not p95, jobs can queue [R9]. You said slow hangs count as failures.
Fix: per-job timeout of 8 minutes for video, 3 minutes for audio, automatic cancel and one retry on another model, async fan-out (the API has no concurrency cap [R9]) so a 10-shot episode renders in about the time of the slowest shot, not the sum. Webhooks are supported [R9 llms index]; polling fallback in Colab.

2.0 Decisions you gave on 8 Oct 2026, 12:48 IST, and what each does to the design

D-2  Language: English primary now. Other languages tested later but "kept in the pipeline". You noted Magic Hour gives 80+ languages. I did not find a language count on the docs pages I read [R7 R9], so treat "80+" as your statement, unverified by me; probe P-6 checks it. Design: every module takes a language code, all prompts and voice choices are parameterised, and the benchmark has a language column.
D-3  Series: "series with great continuity like 21 seasons". This is the heaviest architectural requirement. It adds a persistent Story Bible and continuity engine (Section 2.4), a season and arc planner (2.5), and series-level benchmark metrics (Section 10.6).
D-4  Product to ship, phased: demo now, then scalability, then ship. Section 11 is phased this way, and each phase lists limits and edge cases.
D-5  Credits account: your reply "yes true" did not choose between the two accounts I asked about. Still open (Q5). The key will arrive through a vault link, not chat.

2.1 Design principles
P1  The renderer is a rented commodity; story, shot plan, consistency and measurement are the product.
P2  Every stage has typed input and output (JSON schema), is cached by content hash, and can be rerun alone.
P3  Cheap first: draft, verify, then spend. No unreviewed expensive call.
P4  Decouple audio from video. Decouple identity from motion.
P5  Pluggable everything: LLM, image model, video backend, TTS, lip-sync, judge.
P6  Measure before and after every change on the frozen benchmark.
P7  Fail fast: per-stage timeouts, one retry, then a clear failure record.
P8  Series first: no episode is generated without loading the bible; no episode is accepted without updating it.
P9  Free-only rule: no purchases, no card-gated trials, no paid models outside the existing credits. Official registries only for installs; no piped scripts.

2.2 End-to-end pipeline (stage list)
S0  Intake and normalisation
S1  Series planning (season arc, episode grid)  [series mode only]
S2  Episode brief expansion
S3  Writers' room: beat sheet, script, hook and cliffhanger passes
S4  Script critic loop (rubric + structure checks)
S5  Shot planner and director pass (filmable shots, model-aware coverage)
S6  Character and world asset builder (bible, reference sheets, locations)
S7  Draft render (cheap)
S8  Shot verifier (identity, artifacts, framing)
S9  Final render (selected shots only)
S10 Audio: dialogue TTS or clone, lip-sync, music, ambience, mix
S11 Edit and assemble (cuts, captions, safe areas, loudness)
S12 Reframe and delivery (9:16 master, 16:9 and 1:1 derivatives)
S13 Evaluation (deterministic, judge, human) and logging
S14 Bible update and continuity check  [series mode]
S15 Packaging: episode file, captions, thumbnail, metadata, provenance label

2.3 The writers' room (S2 to S4)
Goal: scripts that are structurally correct and emotionally compelling.
Inputs: user prompt, template, optional required scenes, characters (names, descriptions, images), language, target duration, series context (bible).
Template library (v1 seven genres, my earlier build): hidden identity, revenge, betrayal and affair, contract marriage and CEO romance, werewolf and supernatural, mafia, family and inheritance. Each template stores premise, engine, hook patterns, twist patterns, cliffhanger patterns. Add later: rebirth and time loop, workplace power, campus, Indian family drama and saas-bahu, regional folklore.
Beat structure (60s default): hook 0 to 3s; setup to about 15s; escalation to about 36s; twist at 60 to 85 percent; cliffhanger last 15 percent. Longer episodes scale the same fractions, with a second mini-hook mid-episode for 90 to 120s.
Agents (all prompt roles over one or two LLM calls each, not heavy frameworks):
- Showrunner: owns episode goal, emotional target (who the audience wants punished or rescued), and the one open question the cliffhanger will pose.
- Plotter: beat sheet with plants and payoffs (one planted object, line or name before the twist).
- Dialogue writer: lines under 14 words, 2.6 spoken words per second maximum, in the target language, with subtext through reaction shots.
- Hook doctor: rewrites the first 3 seconds with 5 alternatives, chooses one by rubric.
- Cliffhanger doctor: 5 alternatives, chooses one that poses a specific open question and does not resolve.
- Continuity editor: checks the draft against the bible (Section 2.4) and flags violations.
Critic loop: structural validator (my earlier validate.py checks: contiguous shots, first shot is hook, last is cliffhanger, duration limits, speaker names valid, words per second) plus LLM rubric judge (8 criteria). Loop up to 3 rounds, keeping the best. Use plan-then-write decomposition, which recent work supports: "Beyond Direct Generation" screenwriting decomposition, Dramaturge iterative refinement, outline stage comparison, PLOTTER graph-based planning [P25 P26 P27 P28]. Abstract-level reading only.
Retrieval of craft: a "hook library" and "cliffhanger library" of 20 to 30 human-made episodes you choose, each deconstructed into beat sheets (hook type, first-3-second image, power shift count, twist type, cliffhanger type, runtime of each beat). The writer agent gets 3 nearest examples by template and tone as few-shot structure, never as text to copy.
Deconstruction method (per reference episode, done by you or by me from public clips with notes only, no copying text): timestamp every shot; label beat; count cuts per 10 seconds; mark emotional peak positions; note the last frame and the open question. Output one JSON per episode. This becomes the training set for the structural priors, and the benchmark reference set (Section 10).

2.4 The Story Bible and continuity engine (for "21 seasons")
Why it exists: video models forget. LLM context windows forget. A 21-season series (hundreds of episodes) needs a database that is the single source of truth.
Storage: SQLite in Colab and Drive (portable, queryable) plus JSON export per episode. Scale-up phase moves to Postgres. A vector index (FAISS or sqlite-vss class, both open source) for semantic recall of past scenes.
Entities:
- Series: title, genre template, tone, language, season count plan, content rating, aspect defaults.
- Character: id, name, aliases, role, arc stage, personality, speech style (vocabulary, catchphrases, language mix), relationships (typed edges with status and since-episode), secrets (who knows what, since when), appearance spec (canonical text identity string), wardrobe states per era, reference image set (front, 3/4, profile, expressions), voice id, age timeline.
- Location: id, description, lighting and palette, reference stills, geography relations.
- Props and plot objects: ownership and location over time.
- World state: timeline of facts with episode id. Facts have visibility (public, known-to-set, secret).
- Threads: open plot threads with id, setup episode, promise, planned payoff window, status (open, advanced, resolved, abandoned).
- Cliffhanger ledger: each episode's final open question, whether it is resolved, and in which episode. Rule: every cliffhanger gets resolved within 1 to 3 episodes, and resolution should open a new one.
- Episode log: script, shot list, summary (300 tokens), emotional curve, hook type, twist type, runtime, benchmark scores, credits spent.
- Style bible: color grade, lens language, music motifs, title card, captions style.
Operations:
- load_context(episode_n): retrieves a compact context pack: recap of last 3 episodes, active threads, character states for characters in scope, relevant relationships and secrets, the previous cliffhanger to resolve, and the next planned beats from the season arc. Hard cap about 6k tokens so cost stays flat as the series grows.
- continuity_check(script): rule-based checks (dead characters do not appear, secrets are not mentioned by characters who do not know them, injuries and outfits persist, props are where they should be, locations exist) plus LLM audit; returns violations with fix suggestions.
- commit(episode): update world state, threads, ledger, relationships, character arc stages from the accepted script, with a diff the user can review.
- retcon(request): explicit command that edits past facts with a changelog, because stories sometimes change.
Season and arc planner (S1): creates a series skeleton: premise, 5 to 10 major arcs, season finales, recurring villain escalations, a character growth plan, and a grid of episodes per season with the hook and cliffhanger type targeted for each. Season-long reveals are scheduled and tracked in Threads so they are planted early and paid late. Pacing guard: no more than 3 consecutive episodes of the same twist type; at least one status reversal per episode; every 10 episodes a "mini finale".
Visual continuity across seasons: each character's appearance spec has era versions (for example season 1, season 5 after time skip), each with its own reference sheet; ageing is handled by explicit era assets, not by hoping the model ages the face.
Drift management for very long series: re-anchor character reference sheets every season; keep the original season-1 canonical images as the identity ground truth; run an identity drift benchmark (similarity of newest episode's character crops to season-1 canon) and flag when it falls below a threshold (Section 10.6).
Edge cases in series mode (also in Section 11.5): actor swap (reference image changes), time skips, flashbacks (past-era assets), twins and doubles (identity collision), cameo characters, mid-series genre shifts, user edits to canon, parallel storylines, continuity of language style per character, recaps for new viewers, content rating drift.

2.5 Character reference handling (S6)
Input: 1 to 6 images per character from the user, any quality. The system:
1. Validates each image: face count, resolution, blur, occlusion, aspect, NSFW and real-person flags. Image validation uses small open source detectors (face detection, blur variance, simple classifiers), run locally on CPU in Colab.
2. Consent gate: for photos of real people, require a recorded attestation by the uploader; block known public figures through a face-match against a celebrity watchlist is a possible later feature (needs a careful free dataset review) and is a policy item, not v1.
3. Builds a character sheet: canonical front face, 3/4 left and right, profile, full body, and 4 expressions (neutral, angry, tearful, smirk). Uses Magic Hour image tools (image editor and generator models listed in R3, such as nano-banana variants, seedream, gpt-image, qwen-edit, flux-2-klein; cost 5 to 150 credits each at lowest resolution) or, as free alternatives, open source image editors (Section 7) run elsewhere. The credit cost of a full sheet at 6 images is about 300 credits at 50 credits each, which is why sheets are built once per character and cached.
4. Stores the identity string (canonical text description), the reference file paths in Magic Hour (via upload URLs and file_path) and in the bible.
5. Embeds identity: computes a face embedding with an open source face recognition model (InsightFace class, check licence for commercial use because some model weights are research-only) to use in verification; store only embeddings and hashes unless the user opts to keep images.
Usage at shot time: the shot planner lists characters present; the render stage passes their reference images (references array, or first-frame image) and the identity strings; the verifier compares output face crops against embeddings.
Fallback if references are weak: use a two-step image-first flow: generate the keyframe with an image editor given references (this is stronger at identity), then animate with image-to-video.

2.6 Shot planner and director pass (S5)
Output per shot (extending my existing schema): idx, start, duration, beat, location id, characters present with wardrobe state, first-frame description, motion description, camera (shot size, angle, movement), lighting, dialogue (speaker, text, language, emotion), sfx and music cue, transition, "risk flags" (crowd, hands, text on screen, fast action, rain, mirrors, two-shot with similar faces), and a chosen render mode.
Render mode per shot:
- RM-A: image-to-video from a verified keyframe (default for close-ups and dialogue).
- RM-B: text-to-video with references (establishing and mid shots with one or two characters).
- RM-C: first and last frame interpolation for transitions and controlled moves (image-to-video with end image) [R2].
- RM-D: insert shots (objects, texts, phones) generated from an image tool plus subtle motion; cheapest and safest.
Director pass: rewrites risky shots into safer coverage (replace crowd shot with over-shoulder, avoid hands in focus, keep text on screen as a post overlay instead of generated text).
Coverage rules for microdrama grammar: average shot 3 to 6 seconds; push-in on reveals; reaction shots; hold after the twist; last shot is a freeze or hard cut on a face or object; on-screen captions in safe area (Section 2.9).

2.7 Rendering (S7 to S9) on Magic Hour
Draft model: ltx-2.5 at 480p (24 credits/s) with audio off; used to test composition and action, not final identity.
Final model options (all pinned by ID): kling-3.0 (cinematic stories, references, optional audio) at 720p; seedance-2.0 or 2.5 where reference control matters and budget allows; veo3.1-lite for balanced realism; minimax-h3 or wan-3.0 for longer clips with native audio. Per-shot model routing table is learned from the benchmark (which model has the best identity score per credit for which shot type).
Job control: asynchronous submission, webhook or poll, 8 minute timeout, one retry on a different seed or model, record credits_charged from the finished job, cache by hash, refund awareness (failed jobs are refunded [R1]).
Budget governor: a hard per-episode credit ceiling and a global ceiling read from GET /v1/account; refuse to start a job that would breach it; produce a cost estimate before every batch for your approval in demo phase.
Mock mode: the dry-run backend I already built plus Magic Hour's mock server [R3] so the pipeline and the benchmark run with zero credits.

2.9 Aspect ratio and "auto adjust per device orientation" (D-1)
Interpretation (please confirm in Q1): the primary deliverable is vertical 9:16 for phones; the player should also look right when a viewer holds the phone sideways, on tablets and on desktop.
Plan:
1. Generate the master in 9:16 only (cheapest, and the main use). Magic Hour exposes 9:16 and 16:9 per model [R1].
2. Safe-area composition rule in the shot planner: faces and key action inside the central 1:1 region, so a 1:1 crop works, and in the central 80 percent width for captions.
3. Derive 16:9 by one of: (a) letterbox with a blurred-background fill (cheap, standard, acceptable), (b) smart crop of a separately rendered 16:9 version for hero shots only (costs credits again), (c) outpaint sides using a video outpainting model (research stage; not v1).
4. Delivery manifest: one episode, three renditions (9:16, 1:1, 16:9) plus a JSON of safe areas and caption positions. The player selects by orientation; the web player or app handles auto-switch. In Colab demo we ship the 9:16 file and one 16:9 letterboxed file.
5. Captions: burned-in for social, sidecar (WebVTT or SRT) for product; Magic Hour has an auto subtitle endpoint at about 4.8 credits/s [R3]; open source Whisper-class transcription is a free alternative for timing.
Trade-off to call out: a native 16:9 render per episode doubles the video cost; I recommend not doing that in v1.

2.10 Audio (S10)
Dialogue: TTS or clone per character with a fixed voice id from the bible. Sources: Magic Hour voice generator or cloner [R3 R7], or open source (Section 7). Languages: English first, Hindi next; pronounce names via a pronunciation lexicon in the bible.
Lip sync: only for close-ups with speech. Magic Hour lip sync is 1 credit per frame (about 24 credits/s) and lite or pro modes [R3 R6]. If a model's native audio already syncs well in the probe, skip separate lip sync.
Music and ambience: free licensed sources only; generate or select loops with attribution; music motifs per series in the style bible; sidechain duck under dialogue; target loudness about -14 LUFS for social (my engineering default, not sourced).
Mix: FFmpeg in Colab (official apt package), no external scripts.

2.11 Editing and assembly (S11)

Generate thumbnail from the hook frame.

3. ALTERNATE APPROACHES COMPARED
================================================================================

Eight pipeline families. Scores 1 to 5 (5 best) are my judgment from the evidence in Sections 5 to 7 and the Magic Hour facts; they are not measurements. "Fit" means fit to your constraints: Python in Colab, Magic Hour credits, free-only, series continuity, benchmark-first.

ALT-1  Prompt-per-clip chain (the simple baseline)
What: LLM writes N shot prompts, each goes to text-to-video, clips are concatenated.
Pros: fastest to build (one day), cheapest to debug, good as the control arm of the benchmark.
Cons: identity drift, no reference control, voices differ per clip.
Cost per 60s at ltx-2.5 720p: 2,880 credits at 1.0x.
Consistency 1, Story 2, Cost 4, Build effort 5, Fit 3. Use as baseline B0 in the benchmark.

ALT-2  Keyframe-first (image then animate)  [recommended core]
What: for each shot make a keyframe with an image model using the character references, check identity on the still (cheap: 5 to 150 credits), then image-to-video from the approved keyframe, with optional end frame [R2 R3].
Pros: identity is verified before video credits are spent; still images are cheap to reroll; end-frame control gives continuity between shots.
Cons: more stages; image-to-video can still drift during motion; the keyframe step adds cost (50 credits per image at nano-banana level) but saves video rerolls.
Consistency 4, Story 4, Cost 4, Effort 3, Fit 5.

ALT-3  Reference-to-video (references array) end to end
What: text-to-video with the character references on every shot [R1].
Pros: simplest consistent pipeline if the model honours references; kling-3.0 and seedance-2.0 are marketed for references [R1 R3].
Cons: unknown reference limits (U1); identity bleed between two similar references; costs premium rates for seedance.
Consistency 3 (unverified), Story 4, Cost 2 to 4 depending on model, Effort 4, Fit 4. Test in probes P-1 and P-3, then combine with ALT-2 as render mode RM-B.

ALT-4  Audio-first (voice then visuals)
What: write and record dialogue first, then use audio-to-video or lip-sync to place faces to speech [R6 R8].
Pros: voices consistent by construction; timing fits the audio; good for dialogue-heavy episodes, which most microdramas are.
Cons: audio-to-video output is a generated scene guided by audio, with less control over blocking; lip-sync adds cost; lip-sync on wide shots is poor.
Consistency 3 (voice 5, face 3), Story 4, Cost 3, Effort 3, Fit 4. Use as render mode for dialogue close-ups.

ALT-5  Agentic end-to-end frameworks (ViMax, MAViS, Co-Director, CineCrew, Wind Comic, Jellyfish)
What: adopt a research or open source multi-agent film framework and point it at the Magic Hour backend [P12 to P18; R54 R55 R59].
Pros: fast start on ideas (shot memory, critique loops).
Cons: code quality varies, most target other backends, licence and safety review needed, little regard for budget limits or benchmarking, hard to guarantee fail-fast and no-comment notebook rules.
Consistency 3, Story 3, Cost 2 (agents burn calls), Effort 3, Fit 3. Borrow ideas; do not adopt wholesale. Use as comparison arm B2 in the benchmark if one can be wired in cheaply.

ALT-6  Animated, comic-style (manhua and "AI comic drama")
What: stylised 2D or 3D animation, stills with motion, voiceover. The Chinese market segment of AI comic dramas ("漫剧") is estimated at over 40 billion yuan within a 121 billion yuan 2026 market by DataEye [R17], and Xinhua reports over 10,000 AI comic-style micro dramas released monthly since early 2026 [R20].
Pros: lower consistency demands (stylised faces are easier to keep consistent), lower cost (image to short motion), forgiving of lip-sync errors, big proven audience in China.
Cons: you asked for dramas as engaging as live-action microdramas; stylisation may or may not match your taste; Western microdrama audiences are live-action centred (ReelShort, DramaBox) [R13 R14].
Consistency 4, Story 4, Cost 5, Effort 3, Fit 4. Strong candidate for the cheapest path to a convincing series. Add a style switch: "photoreal" or "comic".

ALT-7  Open source local stack (Wan 2.2 or LTX or HunyuanVideo with LoRA, ComfyUI)
What: self-host video models and train character LoRAs for identity.
Pros: free of API credits, controllable identity via LoRA training, full pipeline ownership [R50 R51 R53].
Cons: needs 24 to 48 GB VRAM for good quality [R51], more than free Colab gives; training and ComfyUI plumbing is heavy; you said "good output or no output" and "fail fast".
Consistency 4 (with LoRA), Story 3, Cost 5 (after hardware), Effort 1, Fit 2 right now. Keep as the scale-phase option.

ALT-8  Hybrid live-footage plus AI (real actors, AI for B-roll, dubbing, localisation)
What: human-shot core with AI for localisation and extra shots; the Indian market runs a lot of human-made microdramas on very low budgets (Alvarez and Marsal commentary on a sub-two-lakh production model) [R83].
Pros: quality ceiling highest; avoids consistency problems.
Cons: not what you asked for; needs actors and crews.
Fit 1. Mentioned for completeness.

6.1 Method for the similarity score (so you can audit it)
1. Define seven dimensions that describe your idea (Table S1), with weights summing to 100. Weights reflect your brief: story engine and consistency and finished video each carry 20, genre control 12, benchmark loop 12 (you stressed it), prompt-led input 8, series continuity 8.

3. Similarity = sum of weight x score / 3, from 0 to 100.
4. Confidence tag: high, med, low based on how much I read.
5. A score of 80 does not mean the product is as good. It means its described scope overlaps yours.
Limits: marketing pages overstate; several are SEO sites (grade C) that may be thin wrappers; I may have missed products in other languages; Chinese platform internals are known mainly through press (Caixin, Xinhua), not product pages.

6.4 What this landscape implies for you (judgment)
1. The category "prompt or script in, short drama out" is crowded. Expect your v1 to match features, not differentiate on them.
2. Nobody visible advertises (a) same-input regression benchmarks, (b) a persistent multi-season bible, (c) per-shot cost governance, (d) Indian language depth. Those are your openings. (b) is directly your "21 seasons" requirement.
3. The best-funded actors (ByteDance, ReelShort) are vertically integrated: IP, models, distribution. A generator without distribution has a harder path to revenue; selling to creators and studios, or feeding one platform, is more realistic than building a consumer app first.
4. Price points: some AI-drama services quote about 10 credits per second of video (Drama.Land page, estimate) [R42]; Lume Studio quotes $400 to $1,200 per 1 to 2 minute AI episode in 4K as a service (vendor, grade C) [R25]; reported Chinese AI series cost about 50,000 yuan (about $7,400) per series versus 300,000 to 800,000 yuan for a 100-episode live-action series in one Chinese district [R21 R89]. 10. QUALITY EVALUATION AND BENCHMARK DESIGN
================================================================================

Your test: "make something you would actually watch, relative to other microdramas and shorts", judged on consistency of voice and video graphics, art style and overall parameters, and "will I enjoy it?". The benchmark turns that into a repeatable instrument. A first harness (structural checks, LLM-judge rubric, human CSV, version comparison) already exists from my first build; this section extends it.

10.1 Principles
1. Same inputs, every time: a frozen case set, frozen seeds where the API allows, frozen judge prompts and judge model IDs.
2. Separate what we change (pipeline version) from what we hold fixed (cases, rubric, judges).
3. Three layers of evidence: deterministic metrics, model judges, human judgments. Report all three; decide on humans.
4. Cost is a metric: credits per accepted second and wall-clock per episode.
5. Report noise: repeat judge calls 3 times, report mean and standard deviation, and mark deltas smaller than noise as ties.
6. Anchors: always include two reference clips (a human-made microdrama you pick, and a deliberately weak AI baseline B0) so the scale cannot drift.

10.2 Benchmark case set (frozen v1, 12 cases)
 C01 hidden identity, 60s, English, 2 characters
 C02 revenge, 60s, English, 3 characters
 C03 betrayal, 60s, English, 3 characters, dialogue heavy
 C04 CEO romance, 60s, English, 2 characters, close-ups
 C05 supernatural, 60s, English, 3 characters, visual effects risk
 C06 mafia, 60s, English, 4 characters, group scene risk
 C07 family inheritance, 90s, English, 5 characters (ensemble)
 C08 hidden identity, 60s, Hindi (experimental until audio probe passes)
 C09 series test: episode 2 of C01 (must resolve C01's cliffhanger)
 C10 series test: episode 3 of C01 after a time skip (era assets)
 C11 user-supplied reference photos (consented), 60s
 C12 user-specified required scenes (3 scenes must appear in order)
Starting subset for the 20k budget: C01, C03, C09 only at draft grade (Phase 2 spends about 5,000 credits).

10.3 Layer 1: deterministic metrics (no judgment)
Structure (script): beat coverage, hook in first 3 seconds, last shot is cliffhanger, shot durations within 2 to 8s, words per second at most 2.6, line length at most 14 words, speaker validity, required scenes present in order.
Identity (video): face embedding cosine similarity of each shot's main face to the character's reference embedding; per-character mean and minimum; across-shot pair similarity; flag below 0.5 (initial guess; calibrate on your accepted shots). Metric source ideas: P1 P4 P50.
Style: colour histogram distance between shots in a scene, palette drift across the episode; VBench style dimensions where applicable [P48].
Motion and artefacts: frame difference spikes, black frames, frozen frames, flicker, text artefacts (OCR detects unwanted text), aspect and resolution compliance.
Audio: integrated loudness near target, clipping, silence gaps longer than 0.8s inside dialogue, speaker embedding similarity between lines by the same character (ECAPA class), word error rate of ASR on dialogue versus script (intelligibility), lip-sync offset estimate when measurable.
Editing rhythm: cuts per 10 seconds, shot length distribution compared to the reference set.
Cost: credits_charged sum per accepted episode, per accepted second, generation wall-clock, retries.

10.4 Layer 2: model judges
Script judge (8 criteria, exists): hook, emotional target, escalation, twist, cliffhanger, dialogue, juice, visual filmability. Temperature 0, 3 repeats, report mean and spread. Pairwise mode: judge sees script A and B in random order, picks one and explains in 2 sentences; run both orders to cancel position bias.
Video judge: a multimodal model watches sampled frames plus the audio transcript (avoid claiming it hears tone): criteria voice consistency (from transcript speaker labels plus the audio metrics), character consistency, art style consistency, lip-sync, pacing, watchability. Because long-video judging reliability is an open question [P45], judge per 15 to 20 second segment, then aggregate, and rely on the judge only for dimensions that correlate with your ratings (10.6).
Rubric sources: RAVEN-Eval, VideoArgus, FIRM-Video, DirectorBench ideas [P40 P41 P42 P43]; short drama quality assessment [P46] to be read in full.

10.5 Layer 3: human evaluation (you)
Time-boxed and cheap: 
- Pairwise: for each release candidate, 6 blind A versus B comparisons (candidate vs previous best) on random cases; you pick the one you would keep watching and tag the reason (hook, story, faces, voice, style, pacing). Under 15 minutes per release.
- Absolute: 1 to 10 "would I enjoy it" and 1 to 5 on the video rubric for 3 cases per release (the human_scoring.csv exists).
- Binary "would I keep watching to the end", and "would I tap next episode" (the format's core outcome).
- Optional external raters: 3 friends from your target audience for the hero episode, 10 minutes each; log age band and microdrama familiarity.
Inter-rater reliability only matters if you add raters; otherwise log your own test-retest: rate 3 old clips again after 3 days.

10.6 Calibration, series metrics and acceptance gates
Calibration: after the first 20 pairwise comparisons, compute Spearman rank correlation between each automatic metric or judge criterion and your preference margin. Keep criteria with correlation above 0.6, down-weight the rest, and publish the weights with the benchmark version.
Composite score (initial, to be recalibrated): 35 percent human pairwise win rate vs previous best, 20 percent identity and style consistency, 15 percent audio (voice consistency plus intelligibility), 15 percent script judge, 10 percent structure compliance, 5 percent cost efficiency. This is a starting guess, not a validated formula.
Series metrics (for the 21-season requirement):
- Canon violations per episode found by the continuity checker (target 0 on rule checks).
- Cliffhanger resolution rate and latency (resolved within 3 episodes).
- Thread health: percentage of open threads advanced within 5 episodes; abandoned threads count.
- Identity drift: similarity of newest episode character crops to the season-1 canonical references, tracked per season (alert threshold set after the first season).
- Voice drift: speaker similarity to the season-1 voice sample.
- Repetition index: n-gram and structure similarity between recent episodes (avoid "mass-produced" patterns [R76]).
Acceptance gates:
- Demo gate: composite improves over B0 by a margin above noise; no identity score below threshold in more than 10 percent of shots; you rate "would I enjoy it" at 7 or higher on 2 of 3 cases.
- Scale gate: cost per accepted second within budget; failure rate under 5 percent per job after one retry; benchmark runs reproducible within noise.
- Ship gate: three external raters rate hero episode 7 or higher; legal and labelling checklist passed; moderation and takedown flow tested.

10.7 Reporting
Each benchmark run emits: run id, pipeline version, git hash, model IDs and docs snapshot date, case results, charts (score by dimension; cost vs score; identity by shot index), an HTML report in Drive, a CSV for the data analysis in Section 8.7, and a plain-language note of "what got better, what got worse, what is within noise".

10.8 Failure catalogue and regression
Keep a growing list of failures (hands, crowds, text, mirrored faces, fast action, rain) with minimal repro prompts. Every release runs the catalogue at the cheapest tier to check for regressions in prompting rules.

10.9 Known weak points of this benchmark
- n=1 human rater unless you add others; mitigated by pairwise design.
- LLM judges prefer fluent, formulaic scripts (my expectation; not verified here); the human layer overrides.
- Face similarity metrics are weaker for stylised or comic characters, for heavy makeup, and for profile views.
- Budget limits the case count; the draft grade may not predict final-grade quality.

14.3 Writers' room system prompt skeleton (kept short, language parameterised)
"You are the showrunner of {series}. Write episode {n} in {language}. Context pack: {recap}, {active_threads}, {character_states}, {previous_cliffhanger}. Plan: {beat_targets}. Rules: hook within 3 seconds; one power reversal; one planted detail paid off; lines under 14 words; end on a specific open question that is not answered. Return JSON matching the schema. Do not contradict canon facts listed."

14.4 Continuity check prompt skeleton
"Given canon facts, secrets and character states, list every contradiction in this script. For each: shot id, rule broken, evidence, minimal fix. Return JSON array; empty if none."

14.5 Judge prompt skeleton (frozen per benchmark version)
"You are a strict viewer of vertical microdramas. Score 1 to 10 on: hook, emotional target, escalation, twist, cliffhanger, dialogue, juice, filmability. Most scripts deserve 4 to 6. Return JSON with scores, weakest criterion and one fix."

14.6 Credit estimator (pseudo)
estimate(shot) = rate[model][res] x dur + audio_surcharge[model] x dur (if native audio on); lip_sync = 24 x dur (standard); tts = ceil(0.1 x chars); image = per-model credits. Governor refuses when sum(planned) + spent > ceiling.

14.7 Notebook outline (Run All clean)
1 Intro and architecture (markdown). 2 Install pinned packages from PyPI. 3 Key via getpass. 4 Account check and budget ceiling. 5 Load or create series. 6 Brief and characters. 7 Writers' room. 8 Shot plan and cost estimate (stop for approval). 9 Draft render. 10 Verify. 11 Final render. 12 Audio and assembly. 13 Evaluation report. 14 Bible commit. Every code cell has no comments; each step has a markdown cell explaining it.
